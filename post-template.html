What does it mean to learn with an AI, not from one?

A reflection on the difference between using AI as a tutor and using it as a thinking partner.

P
Patricia Cruz MSc Computational Social Science · Linköping University

There is a difference between asking someone for an answer and thinking out loud with them. The first is efficient; the second is generative. When I started using large language models in my own studies, I noticed something uncomfortable: I kept treating them like a search engine with better grammar. I was asking for answers. I was not thinking.

The question behind the question

Most of the discourse around AI in education centres on what students get from AI — explanations, feedback, summaries. But there is a more interesting question: what does the act of interacting with AI do to a student's thinking? Does it scaffold cognition, or does it short-circuit it?

This is not a new tension. It mirrors debates about calculators, spell-checkers, and Wikipedia. But generative AI raises the stakes considerably, because it does not just provide information — it produces reasoning, and that reasoning can become a substitute for the student's own.

"The risk is not that students will use AI to cheat. It is that they will use it to think — and never notice they have stopped thinking for themselves."

— Patricia Cruz

What "learning with" could mean

I want to propose a distinction. Learning from AI treats the system as an authority: the student poses a question, receives an answer, and updates their knowledge accordingly. This is efficient, but it is cognitively passive in a specific way — it does not require the student to generate hypotheses, notice contradictions, or struggle productively.

Learning with AI, by contrast, treats the system as an interlocutor: the student externalises their thinking, receives a response that challenges or extends it, and is pushed to refine their own position. The student remains the epistemic agent. The AI is a mirror, not an oracle.

Research note

This distinction maps loosely onto Vygotsky's zone of proximal development — but the key question is whether AI interaction can genuinely function as scaffolding, or whether it removes the productive struggle that makes scaffolding effective.

What I observed in my own work

In a data analysis course last semester, I began keeping logs of my conversations with a language model. Reading them back was revelatory — not because of what the AI said, but because of what I asked. The questions I posed early in a problem were vague and outcome-seeking. Later, as I understood more, they became sharper, more exploratory. The log became a record of my thinking, not just the AI's output.

This is the methodological intuition behind my current research interest: that AI interaction logs might function as a novel kind of cognitive artefact — a trace of reasoning that students and researchers could use to study learning in real time.

I do not have conclusions yet. This is an essay, not a paper — a way of thinking in public about questions I am still working through. If this resonates with your own experience as a learner, educator, or researcher, I would genuinely like to hear from you.
